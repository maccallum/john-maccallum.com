About

John MacCallum is a composer, media artist, and software engineer. Born in California and trained in music composition and computer science, he lived in Berlin from 2019 to 2025 and is now based in Falmouth, UK. His work uses technology to probe our relationship to time. At its center is the heart, as timekeeper, material performer, and world-builder: performers wear electrocardiograms, the heartbeat stands in for the click, and the regular time of the machine is handed over to the heart, which has a fundamentally different notion of regularity. His notated works, among them Aberration, …delicate texture of time…, Hyphos, and Limn, put players in independent, smoothly varying tempos; the performance works extend the same question to bodies, sensors, and machines.

For over a decade that work has been made in collaboration with choreographer and media artist Teoma Naccarato. Together they co-direct III and Im/mediations, two bodies of work comprising eighteen pieces since 2014: extended-duration performances inside high-channel-count real-time audio/visual systems that put philosophical pressure on notions of representation, boundaries, and binaries. In III: Once Returned (2022) they performed a six-hour choreography twelve times in succession over seventy-two hours, inside an array of twelve cameras that live-streamed it. Supported by grants from the German Federal Government Commissioner for Culture and the Media, the work has been presented in Canada, the United States, the United Kingdom, and across Europe, and Naccarato and MacCallum have written about it in Performance Research, Performance Philosophy, TEMPO, and Leonardo.

MacCallum designs and builds the software these systems run on, as he has throughout his career. At UC Berkeley’s Center for New Music and Audio Technologies he co-designed odot with Adrian Freed, a programming environment for OpenSoundControl for which he wrote the C implementation, the expression language, and much of the Max library; it is in the curricula at Berkeley, Arizona State University, and the Hochschule für Musik und Theater Hamburg. Its successor, asoma, an independent project with a first public release planned for 2027, lets a distributed system of sensors, computers, and cameras be addressed as a single instrument. Since 2022 he has worked as an engineer on Max and Max for Live at Cycling ’74 and Ableton. The music drives the software, and the software, in turn, the music.

He holds a PhD in music composition from UC Berkeley, where he was CNMAT’s Musical Systems Designer from 2008 to 2011, and degrees in composition from McGill University and the University of the Pacific. He held postdoctoral research positions in computer science at the TerraSwarm Research Center (UC Berkeley) and at Inria / CNRS / Université Paris-Saclay, and was Lecturer and Researcher at the Hochschule für Musik und Theater Hamburg from 2019 to 2022.

On synchrony

One preoccupation of mine for a long time now has been the idea that simultaneity doesn’t exist in the world, but that in many fields like music and dance, it’s far too important of a concept to not exist. But in those fields then, we must be operating under a different definition of synchronization and simultaneity than the one understood in physics. Importantly, for all of these fields, the concepts of synchronization and simultaneity have temporal extent. The question then is how does that extent get determined, such that its constituent parts can be said to be operating “together” under certain circumstances, and “not together” under others. In fields like telecommunications, they use the notion of “plesiochrony”, or “nearly in time” to refer to these systems. A system is considered plesiochronous when certain explicit limits have been placed on it, and those limits are determined by looking at the needs of certain tasks. So, if the system is operating within those limits, the tasks are able to be accomplished, and we can say that for all intents and purposes, our system is synchronous. It is similar in music; synchrony and simultaneity are not merely issues of the limits of our ability to discriminate tiny temporal misalignments, they are matters of aesthetics (and also ethics, but we will leave that aside for now). The criteria by which we judge the temporal acuity of musicians playing the Rite of Spring are not the same as those we apply to a free jazz combo. Now, you might say that I’m simply mistaking synchronization and simultaneity for something else, something aesthetic, but remember our first point: synchronization and simultaneity don’t exist objectively, they are always matters of aesthetics. Of course we could say that objectively speaking, this or that group of musicians could play together “tighter”, or this or that group of dancers could be more “together”, we could use a variety of tools to measure their “togetherness”, but that is simply the application of one value system to a context where it may not belong. The construction of this context is composition, it is choreography, and the construction of what synchronization and simultaneity mean in a given context are acts of choreography and composition.

In some early works of mine, I began to experiment with musicians playing in continually-varying tempos, independently of one another. I would construct the “tempo maps” ahead of time so that I knew the relationship of all the parts during the compositional process, which let me compose vertically across the parts, to construct virtual tempos that would emerge out of a complex, polytemporal, varitemporal texture. Pieces like this require click-tracks to keep the musicians “together”, however, the temporal extent of “togetherness” has been widened considerably.
